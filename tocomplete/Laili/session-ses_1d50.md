The system architecture is designed to optimize food choices and food-service operations simultaneously under health, sustainability, affordability, and operational constraints.

The proposed methodology follows a **design science** approach. The artifact to be designed is a digital platform that integrates data from multiple stakeholders and uses recommendation intelligence to support decision-making at different levels of the campus food ecosystem. 1. **Stakeholder layer**

- **Healthy food researchers** seek evidence for nutrition interventions and behavioral change.

## Methodology

### 1. Research Approach
This study adopts a **design science research** approach to develop and conceptualize a **multi-stakeholder healthy-food ecosystem** for university campuses. The proposed artifact is a digital platform supported by a **Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS)** that coordinates decisions among food consumers, canteens, food providers, healthy food researchers, and raw food suppliers. The purpose of the system is to improve dietary health outcomes while maintaining affordability, operational feasibility, and sustainability across the campus food network [CITATION].

Unlike conventional recommender systems that focus primarily on end-user preferences, the proposed approach treats food recommendation as a **multi-objective, multi-actor coordination problem**, where the interests of several stakeholders must be balanced simultaneously [CITATION].

### 2. System Context and Stakeholder Identification
The university campus is modeled as a bounded food ecosystem in which multiple actors interact through food demand, food provision, sourcing, and knowledge generation. The primary stakeholders include:

- **Consumers**: students, professors, and support staff, who seek affordable, convenient, healthy, and culturally acceptable meals.
- **Canteens**: campus food outlets responsible for menu planning, food preparation, pricing, and waste management.
- **Food providers/caterers**: actors responsible for recipe design, meal production, and service delivery.
- **Healthy food researchers**: stakeholders who analyze dietary behavior, evaluate interventions, and develop nutrition-related evidence.
- **Raw food suppliers**: producers and distributors who provide ingredients subject to seasonality, cost, and logistics constraints.
- **University administration**: institutional actors concerned with public health, sustainability, inclusion, and policy compliance.

Each stakeholder is assumed to possess distinct objectives, constraints, and performance indicators. The methodological premise is that an effective campus food system should not optimize one stakeholder’s outcomes in isolation, but rather seek acceptable trade-offs among all relevant actors [CITATION].

### 3. Conceptual Architecture
The proposed system architecture consists of five interacting layers:

1. **Stakeholder Layer**
2. **Application Layer**
3. **Data and Integration Layer**
4. **Intelligence and Recommendation Layer**
5. **Governance and Evaluation Layer**

This layered architecture allows the ecosystem to capture stakeholder interactions, process operational and behavioral data, generate recommendations, and evaluate system performance in a structured manner.

### 4. Data Collection and Integration
The methodology assumes the creation of a unified data infrastructure integrating heterogeneous data sources from campus food operations. Data categories include:

- **Consumer data**: demographic attributes, dietary restrictions, stated preferences, price sensitivity, health goals, meal ratings, and purchase history.
- **Meal and nutrition data**: recipe composition, ingredient lists, calorie counts, macronutrient and micronutrient values, allergen information, and nutrition quality scores.
- **Operational data**: inventory levels, kitchen capacity, meal preparation times, queue information, sales volumes, and food waste records.
- **Supplier data**: ingredient availability, delivery lead times, source origin, price fluctuations, and seasonal supply patterns.
- **Sustainability data**: carbon footprint estimates, local sourcing indicators, packaging information, and waste-related environmental impact.
- **Research and intervention data**: experiment conditions, nudging mechanisms, participant groups, and observed behavior change outcomes.

These data are integrated through digital interfaces connecting point-of-sale systems, supplier databases, menu management systems, institutional payment systems, and nutrition repositories [CITATION]. The integrated data layer enables real-time decision support as well as longitudinal analysis.

### 5. Stakeholder Utility Modeling
A central methodological step is the formalization of stakeholder utilities. For each stakeholder group, a set of measurable objectives is defined:

- **Consumers**: taste satisfaction, affordability, convenience, dietary suitability, and health alignment.
- **Canteens**: demand predictability, operational simplicity, stock efficiency, and waste reduction.
- **Food providers**: product performance, profitability, and repeat demand.
- **Suppliers**: demand stability, procurement visibility, and efficient inventory turnover.
- **Researchers**: data quality, intervention measurability, and behavioral insight generation.
- **University administration**: health promotion, sustainability performance, inclusion, and policy compliance.

These utilities are represented as stakeholder-specific scoring dimensions. The recommendation process uses these dimensions to assess whether a meal, menu adjustment, or sourcing decision contributes positively to the broader ecosystem [CITATION].

### 6. Recommendation Framework
The recommendation methodology is designed as a hybrid framework with multiple computational modules.

#### 6.1 Consumer-Level Recommendation
At the individual level, the system generates personalized food recommendations using a hybrid recommendation strategy that may combine:

- **Collaborative filtering**, to identify patterns among users with similar preferences or behaviors;
- **Content-based filtering**, to match users with meals sharing desirable nutritional or ingredient characteristics;
- **Context-aware recommendation**, to account for dynamic variables such as time of day, location, schedule pressure, queue length, or special campus events.

This module produces ranked meal options tailored to each consumer’s profile and immediate context [CITATION].

#### 6.2 Multi-Stakeholder Optimization
After candidate meal options are generated, the system applies a **multi-objective optimization process** to ensure that recommendations also satisfy ecosystem-level criteria. Rather than maximizing only individual preference, the system calculates an overall recommendation score based on weighted stakeholder utilities.

A generalized formulation may be expressed as:

\[
Score(i,u) = w_1U_{consumer} + w_2U_{nutrition} + w_3U_{affordability} + w_4U_{canteen} + w_5U_{supplier} + w_6U_{sustainability}
\]

where \(i\) denotes a meal or food-related action, \(u\) denotes a user or decision context, and \(w_1...w_6\) are adjustable weights reflecting institutional priorities. These weights can be adapted to emphasize specific goals, such as affordability during financial hardship periods or sustainability during institutional green initiatives [CITATION].

#### 6.3 Demand Forecasting
To support upstream planning, the system incorporates forecasting models for:

- meal demand,
- time-sensitive purchase peaks,
- ingredient consumption,
- probability of unsold stock and food waste.

Forecasting may be conducted using time-series analysis, regression methods, or machine learning models depending on data availability and operational complexity [CITATION]. Forecast outputs inform both canteen preparation decisions and supplier procurement planning.

#### 6.4 Menu Optimization
Based on forecast, nutrition, and operational data, the system recommends menu modifications such as:

- healthier substitutions,
- seasonal ingredient replacements,
- pricing adjustments,
- production quantity changes,
- repositioning of meals to improve visibility and uptake.

This module ensures that healthy food promotion remains compatible with actual inventory, kitchen capacity, and expected demand.

#### 6.5 Procurement Recommendation
For the supplier side, the system generates sourcing recommendations aligned with predicted ingredient needs, sustainability preferences, and supply constraints. These may include:

- preferred suppliers for specific ingredients,
- shifts toward local or seasonal alternatives,
- early procurement signals based on forecasted demand,
- substitution guidance during shortages or price spikes.

This strengthens coordination between campus demand and upstream supply [CITATION].

#### 6.6 Intervention and Nudge Engine
A dedicated intervention module allows researchers and administrators to test behavioral strategies aimed at encouraging healthier and more sustainable food choices. Interventions may include:

- nutritional labeling,
- healthy-meal discounts,
- social norm messaging,
- default healthy options,
- gamification and loyalty rewards,
- meal placement or digital prominence adjustments.

The system tracks intervention exposure and outcomes, allowing researchers to evaluate their effectiveness over time [CITATION].

### 7. System Outputs
The platform generates differentiated outputs for each stakeholder group:

- **Consumers** receive personalized meal recommendations and nutrition-aware suggestions.
- **Canteens** receive demand forecasts, production guidance, and waste-reduction recommendations.
- **Food providers** receive menu performance analytics and pricing insights.
- **Suppliers** receive ingredient demand forecasts and sourcing signals.

## I. INTRODUCTION
Universities are important institutional food environments in which recurring dietary decisions are embedded within everyday academic life. Students, professors, and support staff frequently rely on campus food services for convenience, affordability, and accessibility, making universities strategic sites for improving dietary behavior and promoting healthier eating patterns [1], [2]. However, campus food systems commonly face persistent constraints, including unhealthy default options, limited personalization, time pressure, budget sensitivity, food waste, and fragmented coordination across supply and service actors [1], [2].

At the same time, universities are increasingly expected to advance broader societal goals, including public health promotion, environmental sustainability, and social inclusion. Recent scholarship on sustainable diets and food environments emphasizes that food systems should be designed to support both nutritional and ecological objectives rather than treating them separately [3], [4]. In the university context, this implies that food-service design must move beyond simple meal provision toward ecosystem-level coordination among demand, supply, operations, and institutional policy.

Existing recommender systems offer useful tools for personalization, but they are typically designed to maximize the preferences of a single stakeholder, most often the end user [5]. Such an approach is insufficient for campus food environments, where food choices and service decisions affect multiple parties simultaneously. A recommendation that is attractive to a student may be operationally difficult for a canteen, unprofitable for a provider, unstable for a supplier, or misaligned with university sustainability goals. This concern is consistent with emerging work on **multi-stakeholder recommendation** and **multi-sided fairness**, which highlights that recommender systems often operate in environments characterized by multiple, interdependent interests [6], [16], [17].

To address this gap, this paper proposes a **multi-stakeholder healthy-food ecosystem** for university campuses, supported by a **Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS)**. The core premise is that campus food recommendation should be treated as a coordination mechanism that simultaneously informs meal choice, menu planning, sourcing decisions, intervention design, and policy oversight. The paper develops a conceptual framework for this ecosystem and outlines its methodological, architectural, and governance components.

The main contribution of this paper is threefold. First, it extends food recommender system thinking from user-level personalization to ecosystem-level coordination. Second, it introduces a layered architecture that integrates recommendation, forecasting, procurement, and sustainability intelligence. Third, it positions the campus food environment as a multi-actor digital system in which health, operational, and sustainability goals must be jointly optimized.

## II. METHODOLOGY

### A. Research Approach
This study adopts a **design science research** approach to develop a conceptual socio-technical artifact for improving university food systems [7]. Design science is appropriate because the aim is not merely to describe existing food-service problems, but to propose a structured and actionable framework capable of guiding future implementation and empirical validation. The artifact developed in this study is a digital ecosystem platform supported by a multi-stakeholder recommendation architecture.

### B. Stakeholder Identification
The proposed ecosystem comprises six principal stakeholder groups:

- **Consumers:** students, professors, and support staff  
- **Canteens:** campus dining operators and food-service managers  
- **Food providers/caterers:** actors involved in meal production and recipe design  
- **Healthy-food researchers:** nutrition and behavioral researchers studying interventions and food choice  
- **Raw-food suppliers:** farms, distributors, and ingredient providers  
- **University administration:** institutional decision-makers responsible for health, sustainability, and compliance

Each stakeholder group is associated with distinct objectives and constraints. Consumers seek convenience, taste, affordability, and dietary suitability. Canteens prioritize demand predictability, kitchen feasibility, and waste reduction. Providers are concerned with menu performance and profitability. Suppliers seek stable and visible demand. Researchers value measurable intervention outcomes and access to reliable behavioral data. Administrators prioritize wellbeing, sustainability, equity, and institutional accountability. Framing the campus food environment in this way is consistent with research conceptualizing recommender systems as inherently multi-stakeholder environments [16], [17].

### C. Conceptual System Architecture
The architecture is structured into five interacting layers:

1. **Stakeholder Layer**  
2. **Application Layer**  
3. **Data and Integration Layer**  
4. **Intelligence and Recommendation Layer**  
5. **Governance and Evaluation Layer**

This layered structure allows the framework to capture actor interactions, manage heterogeneous data, generate decision support, and maintain transparency and institutional control.

### D. Data Collection and Integration
The ecosystem relies on integrating multiple categories of data, including:

- dietary preferences and restrictions  
- purchase history and meal ratings  
- recipe composition and nutritional profiles  
- inventory levels and kitchen capacity  
- supplier availability, price fluctuations, and seasonality  
- food waste records  
- sustainability indicators such as carbon footprint and sourcing profiles  
- intervention and behavioral experiment data

These data sources are linked through interoperable interfaces, such as point-of-sale systems, menu management platforms, supplier information systems, and campus payment infrastructures. This approach is aligned with broader developments in data-driven food system innovation and heterogeneous food-data integration [8].

### E. Recommendation and Optimization Logic
At the consumer level, the system uses a hybrid recommendation strategy combining collaborative filtering, content-based recommendation, and context-aware recommendation. This allows the system to personalize meal suggestions according to prior choices, nutritional preferences, dietary restrictions, budget sensitivity, time constraints, and situational context [9], [11].

However, the proposed framework extends beyond conventional personalization by introducing a second-stage **multi-objective optimization process**. Candidate recommendations are evaluated not only in terms of consumer relevance, but also against operational, supplier, affordability, and sustainability criteria. This logic is informed by recent work on fairness-aware and multi-stakeholder recommender systems, where ranking and re-ranking mechanisms are used to balance the utilities of several affected actors [6], [10], [16].

A generalized scoring function may be expressed as:

\[
Score = w_1C + w_2N + w_3A + w_4O + w_5S + w_6E
\]

where \(C\) represents consumer relevance, \(N\) nutrition quality, \(A\) affordability, \(O\) operational feasibility, \(S\) supplier alignment, and \(E\) environmental sustainability. The weights can be adjusted to reflect institutional priorities, allowing universities to place greater emphasis on affordability, health promotion, or sustainability depending on strategic context.

### F. Supporting Intelligence Modules
To support ecosystem-wide coordination, the recommendation core is complemented by additional modules:

- **Demand Forecasting Module:** predicts meal demand, service peaks, ingredient needs, and waste risk  
- **Menu Optimization Module:** recommends healthier substitutions, price adjustments, and production quantities  
- **Procurement Recommendation Module:** aligns sourcing decisions with forecasted demand and seasonal availability  
- **Intervention Module:** supports the testing of nudges such as labels, discounts, defaults, and social norm cues  
- **Sustainability Scoring Module:** evaluates meals and sourcing choices according to environmental indicators

These modules enable the system to function not simply as a user recommender, but as a broader coordination mechanism across the campus food ecosystem. Their inclusion is supported by prior literature on food waste reduction, digital food-service intelligence, and healthy eating interventions [12], [14].

### G. Governance and Evaluation
Because the proposed system processes behavioral and dietary data while influencing food choice and procurement decisions, governance is a central methodological requirement. The governance layer includes:

- privacy and consent management  
- fairness monitoring  
- explainability mechanisms  
- audit logging  
- institutional policy enforcement

Such controls are essential in multi-stakeholder recommendation settings, where algorithmic outputs may advantage some actors while disadvantaging others unless fairness and transparency are explicitly designed into the system [6], [15], [18].

Evaluation should therefore extend beyond conventional recommendation accuracy. Relevant performance dimensions include healthy meal uptake, user satisfaction, affordability retention, food waste reduction, forecast accuracy, supplier stability, and sustainability performance [1], [12], [14].

## III. PROPOSED FRAMEWORK

### A. Ecosystem Logic
The proposed framework conceptualizes the campus food environment as an interconnected ecosystem in which food-related decisions are distributed across multiple actors rather than concentrated in individual consumers alone. Within this ecosystem, recommendation serves as a coordination mechanism linking downstream demand with upstream operations, sourcing, and policy goals. This perspective addresses a major limitation of user-centric recommender systems and aligns with the view that recommender environments are socially and institutionally embedded [5], [16], [17].

### B. Consumer Interaction Layer
Consumers interact with the system through a mobile application or web interface. Based on individual dietary preferences, health goals, location, budget, schedule, and prior behavior, the platform recommends meal options that are contextually relevant and nutritionally informed. Prior research on food recommender systems suggests that such personalization can improve decision support in complex food-choice environments, particularly when health-related criteria are incorporated [9], [11].

### C. Canteen and Provider Coordination
Canteens and food providers receive dashboard-based decision support concerning menu planning, production scheduling, stock management, and price adjustments. By linking predicted demand with real-time operational constraints, the system can reduce mismatch between preparation and actual sales while increasing the visibility and uptake of healthier meals. This is particularly important in food-service contexts where oversupply and poor planning contribute to avoidable food waste [12].

### D. Supplier Integration
Suppliers are connected through procurement-oriented interfaces that communicate forecasted ingredient demand, seasonal substitutions, and expected delivery volumes. This supports more stable procurement planning and enables greater reliance on local, seasonal, or sustainability-certified ingredients where feasible. Such coordination reflects current discussions on food-system transition, which stress the importance of integrating environmental considerations into everyday food provisioning [3], [4].

### E. Research and Intervention Support
A distinctive feature of the framework is its explicit inclusion of healthy-food researchers as active stakeholders. Researchers can use the platform to implement and evaluate interventions such as nutritional labeling, price incentives, default healthy options, or social norm messaging. By embedding experimentation within operational food-service settings, the system creates opportunities for evidence-based refinement of both digital recommendation strategies and campus nutrition policy. This is supported by meta-analytic evidence showing that healthy eating nudges can be effective when appropriately designed and contextually deployed [14].

## IV. DISCUSSION
The proposed framework contributes to the literature by reframing food recommendation in university settings as an ecosystem coordination problem rather than a purely individual prediction task. This shift is important because campus food decisions are shaped not only by preference, but also by institutional infrastructure, supply conditions, economic constraints, and operational realities. A healthy meal recommendation is valuable only if it is also affordable, available, feasible to prepare, and compatible with broader sustainability goals.

The framework also offers an interdisciplinary bridge across recommender systems, digital public health, food-service management, and sustainable food systems. In doing so, it responds to the growing need for socio-technical models that account for both algorithmic intelligence and institutional context. Rather than treating personalization, procurement, and intervention separately, the model integrates them into a single architecture capable of producing coordinated outcomes [3], [5], [9], [16].

Nevertheless, several challenges remain. First, data integration across food-service, supplier, and behavioral systems may be technically demanding, especially where legacy campus infrastructures are fragmented. Second, stakeholder objectives may be difficult to reconcile algorithmically. For example, the healthiest option may not be the most affordable, and the most sustainable option may not be the most operationally convenient. Third, ethical concerns arise when food choices are shaped through algorithmic nudging, particularly if users lack visibility into how recommendations are generated. These concerns underscore the importance of governance mechanisms grounded in fairness, explainability, and institutional accountability [6], [15], [18].

Despite these limitations, the framework offers substantial practical and theoretical value. Practically, it can help universities redesign campus food services as coordinated, data-informed systems. Theoretically, it expands the scope of multi-stakeholder recommendation research into a domain with strong relevance for public health and sustainability.

# Abstract

University campuses are influential food environments in which everyday food choices shape health, wellbeing, and productivity. However, campus food systems often struggle to reconcile nutritional quality, affordability, operational efficiency, and sustainability. This paper develops a conceptual framework for a **multi-stakeholder healthy-food ecosystem** for university campuses, enabled by a **Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS)**. In contrast to conventional food recommender systems that primarily optimize for end-user utility, the proposed framework incorporates the interests of multiple actors, including students, faculty, support staff, canteens, food providers, healthy-food researchers, raw-food suppliers, and university administrators. The framework integrates personalized recommendation, demand forecasting, menu optimization, procurement coordination, sustainability scoring, and intervention support within a unified digital ecosystem. The paper argues that campus food recommendation should be understood not only as a personalization problem but also as a multi-actor coordination challenge. It contributes a conceptual foundation for future empirical research on intelligent, healthy, and sustainable university food systems.

**Keywords:** multi-stakeholder recommendation; healthy food ecosystem; university campus; food recommendation; sustainability; personalized nutrition

# Introduction

University campuses are important institutional food environments in which recurring dietary decisions are embedded in everyday academic life. Students, professors, and support staff often rely on campus food services because of convenience, affordability, and time pressure, making universities critical sites for health promotion and sustainable food system innovation [@roy2019; @deliens2014]. Yet campus food environments are frequently characterized by unhealthy default options, limited dietary personalization, price sensitivity, operational inefficiencies, and food waste [@roy2019; @deliens2014]. These challenges indicate that campus dining should not be treated merely as a service operation, but as a broader socio-technical system in which multiple actors shape food outcomes.

At the same time, universities are increasingly expected to advance institutional goals related to public health, environmental sustainability, and inclusion. Recent scholarship on sustainable diets and food environments emphasizes that healthier and more sustainable food systems must be designed in integrated ways rather than as separate nutritional and ecological agendas [@vieux2023; @malone2023]. In the university context, this means that food-system design should simultaneously account for consumer choice, canteen operations, supplier coordination, intervention strategies, and policy oversight.

Conventional recommender systems provide useful personalization tools, but they are typically designed to optimize outcomes for a single stakeholder, usually the end user [@ricci2022]. Such a model is insufficient in campus food settings, where recommendations can affect multiple parties simultaneously. A meal recommendation that is attractive to a student may be difficult for a canteen to prepare, inconsistent with supplier availability, unprofitable for a provider, or misaligned with institutional sustainability priorities. This limitation is well aligned with the growing literature on multi-stakeholder recommendation, which argues that recommender systems frequently operate in settings involving multiple, interdependent interests [@burke2017; @abdollahpouri2019ms; @abdollahpouri2017].

This paper responds to that gap by proposing a multi-stakeholder healthy-food ecosystem for university campuses, supported by a Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS). Rather than treating recommendation as a user-only prediction problem, the paper conceptualizes it as a coordination mechanism connecting food demand, menu planning, sourcing, sustainability, and behavioral intervention. The main contribution is a conceptual framework that integrates these dimensions into a coherent architecture for future empirical development.

# Literature Review

## Campus Food Environments

University food environments significantly influence dietary behavior. Research shows that the eating patterns of university students are shaped by affordability, convenience, taste, availability, peer context, and time constraints [@deliens2014]. Tertiary education settings often present barriers to healthy eating, including the prevalence of energy-dense foods, irregular schedules, and limited access to affordable nutritious options [@roy2019]. At the same time, universities are uniquely positioned to implement institutional interventions capable of shaping healthier behavior at scale [@roy2019].

However, much of the campus food literature focuses on behavioral determinants or isolated interventions, rather than on the design of integrated food ecosystems. As a result, the literature provides valuable insight into why unhealthy patterns emerge, but offers fewer system-level frameworks for coordinating the multiple actors involved in campus food provision.

## Food Recommender Systems

Food recommender systems represent a specialized branch of recommender systems research that focuses on supporting meal, recipe, and food-choice decisions [@trattner2017]. Existing studies have applied collaborative filtering, content-based recommendation, and context-aware techniques to food choice problems, including health-aware recommendation [@ge2015]. These systems can help users navigate large food spaces and can incorporate individual preferences, dietary restrictions, and health goals.

Despite these advances, most food recommender systems remain user-centric. They typically optimize for relevance, preference satisfaction, or nutrition fit at the individual level, while paying less attention to institutional and supply-side consequences. This creates a gap in contexts such as campus dining, where food recommendation is embedded in operational and policy structures.

## Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation

The literature on multi-stakeholder recommendation challenges the assumption that recommendation systems should optimize exclusively for users. Instead, it argues that recommendation environments often involve providers, platforms, institutions, and other affected groups whose interests should also be considered [@burke2017; @abdollahpouri2019ms; @abdollahpouri2017]. This perspective is closely related to research on multi-sided fairness, which investigates how recommendation outputs may advantage or disadvantage different parties [@burke2017; @burke2018].

In campus food settings, this perspective is especially valuable. Consumers, canteens, providers, suppliers, and administrators all have legitimate, though not identical, objectives. Therefore, university food recommendation provides a strong applied domain for extending multi-stakeholder recommendation theory.

## Sustainable and Healthy Food Systems

Research on sustainable diets increasingly emphasizes that health and environmental objectives must be addressed together [@vieux2023]. Food-environment scholarship similarly highlights the importance of institutional settings in shaping more sustainable and healthier consumption patterns [@malone2023]. In practice, however, sustainability, nutrition, affordability, and operations are often managed separately, resulting in fragmented decision-making. A more integrated architecture is therefore needed to coordinate these dimensions.

## Research Gap

Taken together, four literature streams are relevant to this study:

1. campus food environments,
2. food recommender systems,
3. multi-stakeholder recommendation,
4. sustainable food systems.

Although each stream provides important insights, they remain weakly integrated in the university food-service context. Specifically, there is limited conceptual work on how a campus food ecosystem could use recommendation intelligence to coordinate multiple actors while balancing health, affordability, feasibility, and sustainability. This paper addresses that gap.

# Theoretical Foundation

## Socio-Technical Systems Perspective

This framework adopts a socio-technical systems perspective, according to which food outcomes are shaped by the interaction of technological systems, organizational processes, stakeholder behavior, and institutional governance. In campus settings, meal choices do not emerge solely from personal preference; they are also shaped by availability, pricing structures, digital interfaces, kitchen operations, and supplier constraints. Therefore, recommendation must be understood as embedded within a broader organizational system rather than as an isolated algorithmic function.

## Multi-Stakeholder Systems Logic

The second theoretical foundation is multi-stakeholder systems logic. In this view, food-service environments are composed of actors with partially aligned and partially conflicting objectives. Consumers may prioritize convenience and taste, canteens may prioritize feasibility and waste reduction, suppliers may prioritize stable demand, and institutions may prioritize health and sustainability. This implies that recommendation should be formulated as a multi-objective coordination problem, rather than a single-utility ranking task [@abdollahpouri2019ms; @abdollahpouri2017].

## Conceptual Premise

The central premise of this paper is that healthier and more sustainable campus food systems can be achieved when recommendation systems are designed not only to match users with meals, but also to coordinate:

- consumer choice,
- menu design,
- canteen operations,
- supplier sourcing,
- behavioral interventions,
- institutional goals.

# Conceptual Framework

## Overview

The proposed framework consists of five interacting layers:

1. Stakeholder Layer
2. Application Layer
3. Data and Integration Layer
4. Intelligence and Recommendation Layer
5. Governance and Evaluation Layer

Together, these layers define a digital ecosystem capable of coordinating food-related decisions across the university campus.

## Stakeholder Layer

The ecosystem includes six primary stakeholder groups:

- consumers (students, professors, support staff),
- canteens,
- food providers/caterers,
- raw-food suppliers,
- healthy-food researchers,
- university administration.

Each group both contributes data and receives decision support. Consumers provide preference, feedback, and consumption data. Canteens contribute operational and inventory data. Providers contribute recipe and pricing information. Suppliers provide availability, cost, and seasonality data. Researchers contribute intervention designs and analytical models. Administrators define policy priorities and performance targets.

## Application Layer

The application layer includes several interfaces:

- a mobile or web app for consumers,
- dashboards for canteens and providers,
- a supplier portal,
- a researcher dashboard,
- an administrative dashboard.

These interfaces ensure that the ecosystem does not deliver recommendations only to end users, but also to operational and policy actors.

## Data and Integration Layer

The framework integrates multiple forms of data:

- dietary restrictions and preferences,
- purchase history and ratings,
- recipe and nutrition data,
- kitchen capacity and inventory,
- supplier availability and price changes,
- food waste logs,
- sustainability indicators,
- intervention and experiment data.

This is consistent with recent work on data-driven food system innovation, which emphasizes the importance of integrating heterogeneous food, nutrition, and supply-chain data [@vanderheijden2020].

## Intelligence and Recommendation Layer

The intelligence layer is the core of the framework and includes:

- a consumer recommendation engine,
- a multi-objective optimization engine,
- a demand forecasting module,
- a menu optimization module,
- a procurement recommendation module,
- an intervention/nudge engine,
- a sustainability scoring engine.

At the consumer level, the framework uses hybrid recommendation techniques combining collaborative filtering, content-based methods, and context-aware recommendation [@trattner2017; @ge2015]. However, recommendation does not stop at personalization. Candidate meals and actions are then evaluated through a multi-objective process that incorporates consumer relevance, nutritional quality, affordability, operational feasibility, supplier alignment, and environmental sustainability.

A generalized scoring function can be expressed as:

$$
Score = w_1C + w_2N + w_3A + w_4O + w_5S + w_6E
$$

where $C$ is consumer relevance, $N$ is nutritional quality, $A$ is affordability, $O$ is operational feasibility, $S$ is supplier alignment, and $E$ is environmental sustainability. The adjustable weights allow institutions to prioritize different strategic goals depending on context.

This approach aligns with multi-stakeholder recommendation research and fairness-aware ranking work, which suggest that recommendation systems can incorporate multiple utilities through ranking and re-ranking mechanisms [@burke2017; @abdollahpouri2019; @abdollahpouri2019ms].

## Governance and Evaluation Layer

The governance layer includes:

- privacy and consent management,
- fairness monitoring,
- explainability,
- audit logging,
- policy enforcement,
- KPI tracking.

These functions are essential because food recommendation systems can shape individual behavior, operational choices, and supplier outcomes. Research on fairness in recommendation and machine learning underscores the importance of transparency and multi-party accountability in such settings [@burke2017; @binns2018; @burke2018].
