\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Mission-oriented service systems pose a fundamental engineering
management challenge because value must be created across multiple
stakeholders with interdependent objectives, constrained resources, and
shared sustainability goals. This paper develops an \textbf{engineering
management framework} for designing and improving
\textbf{mission-oriented sustainable service ecosystems} through
\textbf{value co-creation modeling} and a \textbf{Multi-Stakeholder
Recommendation System (MSRS)} embedded in a broader decision-support
architecture. Rather than treating recommendation as a user-centric
personalization tool, the framework conceptualizes MSRS as a
coordination mechanism that aligns stakeholder decisions across design,
production, delivery, and governance.

The framework is illustrated through the context of \textbf{healthy,
tasty, and affordable campus food services}, where consumers, producers,
operators, and regulators jointly shape service outcomes. The model
integrates four elements: stakeholder utility structures, exchange
mechanisms, food and service flows, and a digital coordination layer
that supports co-design, co-engineering, production planning, delivery
execution, and adaptive improvement. In this setting, the MSRS
coordinates consumer needs and profiles, producer capabilities and
capacities, operator feasibility constraints, and regulatory
requirements to improve ecosystem-level performance.

The paper contributes to engineering management in three principal ways.
First, it extends value co-creation from a descriptive service concept
to a formal design logic for mission-oriented service ecosystems.
Second, it reframes multi-stakeholder recommendation systems as
mechanisms for ecosystem coordination rather than only end-user
recommendation. Third, it provides a simulation-ready framework for
evaluating multidimensional performance outcomes, including service
effectiveness, affordability, operational efficiency, sustainability,
and innovation. The framework offers a transferable basis for managing
sustainable service ecosystems in food and other mission-oriented
industries.

\textbf{Keywords:} engineering management; mission-oriented innovation;
sustainable service ecosystem; value co-creation; multi-stakeholder
recommendation system; decision support system; service innovation;
campus food services; affordability; sustainability

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Mission-oriented service systems pose a distinctive engineering
management challenge because value must be created across multiple
stakeholders with interdependent objectives, constrained resources, and
shared sustainability goals. In such systems, performance depends not
only on the quality of a focal product or service, but also on the
ability to coordinate actors whose goals are partially aligned yet often
conflicting. Consumers seek relevant, affordable, and high-quality
outcomes; producers seek viable and scalable offerings; operators seek
efficient and reliable execution; and regulators seek compliance with
broader public or institutional missions such as sustainability, health,
inclusion, and fairness. As a result, managing mission-oriented services
requires more than optimizing isolated functions. It requires
ecosystem-level coordination across design, production, delivery, and
governance.

This challenge is particularly visible in service ecosystems where
mission achievement depends simultaneously on market responsiveness and
public-value creation. Conventional service design approaches often
treat innovation as a sequence led by a focal provider, while
conventional recommendation systems typically optimize for end-user
relevance alone. Neither perspective is sufficient when sustainable
performance depends on coordinated exchanges among heterogeneous
stakeholders. In such contexts, value is not produced by one actor and
delivered to another; it is co-created through the integration of demand
signals, production capabilities, operational constraints, regulatory
requirements, and continuous feedback.

This paper addresses that problem by proposing a \textbf{value
co-creation modeling framework} for \textbf{mission-oriented sustainable
service ecosystems} supported by a \textbf{Multi-Stakeholder
Recommendation System (MSRS)}. The core argument is that MSRS should be
understood not merely as a recommendation tool, but as a digital
coordination mechanism that helps align stakeholder decisions with
mission-level objectives. The framework focuses on four stakeholder
groups---\textbf{consumers, producers, operators, and regulators}---and
explains how their interactions can be structured through exchange
mechanisms that support co-design, co-engineering, production, delivery,
consumption, and continuous reconfiguration.

The framework is illustrated through the case of \textbf{healthy, tasty,
and affordable campus food services}, which provides a bounded yet
representative setting for examining mission-oriented service design.
This context is particularly suitable because campus food services
combine product and service design, operational execution,
sustainability pressures, affordability constraints, and institutional
mission goals. In this setting, consumers shape demand through
preferences and profiles, producers shape feasible offerings through
capabilities and innovation options, operators shape service performance
through capacity and delivery decisions, and regulators shape mission
alignment through standards, incentives, and governance. The proposed
MSRS coordinates these interactions by balancing stakeholder utilities
rather than privileging a single actor's objective.

The paper contributes to engineering management in three principal ways.
First, it develops a mission-oriented view of value co-creation in which
stakeholder alignment is treated as a design problem rather than an
emergent by-product of service exchange. Second, it reframes MSRS as a
mechanism for ecosystem orchestration that connects stakeholder
utilities to mission performance across the full service lifecycle.
Third, it offers a simulation-ready framework for evaluating ecosystem
performance in terms of effectiveness, affordability, operational
efficiency, sustainability, and innovation. By doing so, the paper
provides an engineering management perspective on how sustainable
service ecosystems can be designed, governed, and improved through
coordinated digital decision support.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops
the theoretical framework and propositions. Section 3 explains the
process model of co-design and co-engineering in mission-oriented
service ecosystems. Section 4 presents the system architecture and
decision-support logic. Section 5 outlines the multi-agent and
simulation-based evaluation design. Section 6 reports illustrative
results. Section 7 discusses theoretical and managerial implications.
The final section concludes and identifies directions for future
research.

\section{Literature Review Design}\label{literature-review-design}

\subsection{Review objective}\label{review-objective}

The review aimed to identify and synthesize research relevant to a
\textbf{healthy-food ecosystem for university campuses using
multi-stakeholder recommendation systems}. Because this exact topic is
still emerging, the review used an interdisciplinary search strategy
spanning four linked domains:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  campus food environments,
\item
  food and healthy-food recommender systems,
\item
  multi-stakeholder recommendation and fairness,
\item
  sustainability and food-service operations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Search strategy}\label{search-strategy}

A rapid systematic review logic was applied using targeted scholarly web
searching across the four domains above. Search terms included
combinations of the following concepts:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  multi-stakeholder recommendation,
\item
  food recommender systems,
\item
  healthy food recommendation,
\item
  campus food environment,
\item
  university food environment,
\item
  university restaurants sustainability,
\item
  food waste in food services.
\end{itemize}

Priority was given to review papers, foundational conceptual papers,
technical studies with clear relevance, and implementation-oriented
studies.

\subsection{Inclusion criteria}\label{inclusion-criteria}

Studies were included if they contributed directly to at least one of
the following:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  university or campus food environments,
\item
  food or healthy-food recommender systems,
\item
  multi-stakeholder recommendation, multi-sided fairness, or
  multi-objective recommendation,
\item
  sustainability, food-service operations, or waste reduction in
  institutional dining.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Exclusion criteria}\label{exclusion-criteria}

Studies were excluded if they:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  were unrelated to food, recommendation, or institutional dining,
\item
  were too general to inform the proposed framework,
\item
  or did not contribute meaningful conceptual, empirical, or
  methodological insight.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Screening and selection
logic}\label{screening-and-selection-logic}

Screening followed a staged logic. First, potentially relevant studies
were identified across the four domains. Second, titles and abstracts or
result snippets were screened for conceptual relevance. Third, full-text
or source-level pages were assessed for eligibility. Studies were
retained if they offered direct evidence on campus food behavior or
institutional food systems, technical or conceptual foundations for
healthy-food recommendation, theoretical grounding for multi-stakeholder
recommendation, or sustainability and operational insights relevant to
food-service coordination.

\subsection{Study Selection Procedure}\label{study-selection-procedure}

This review followed a rapid systematic literature review approach
across four related domains: campus food environments, food and
healthy-food recommender systems, multi-stakeholder recommendation and
fairness, and sustainability or food-service operations. Because the
topic remains emergent and interdisciplinary, the review used a
PRISMA-style flow logic rather than a full database-exhaustive PRISMA
2020 procedure.

\section{Theoretical Background}\label{theoretical-background}

\subsection{Campus food environment
research}\label{campus-food-environment-research}

The campus food environment literature consistently shows that
university food settings shape dietary behavior through availability,
affordability, convenience, social context, and environmental design
(Roy et al. 2015; Deliens et al. 2014; {Caruso et al.} 2025). This
stream establishes the university as a legitimate unit of intervention
and supports the view that food choice is shaped by institutional
conditions rather than personal preference alone.

\subsection{Food and healthy-food recommender
systems}\label{food-and-healthy-food-recommender-systems}

The food recommender systems literature indicates that food
recommendation is now a well-developed subfield with a distinct
methodological identity ({Bondevik et al.} 2024; Tran et al. 2018;
Trattner and Elsweiler 2017). Common methods include content-based
recommendation, collaborative filtering, hybrid models, and
machine-learning approaches. However, most systems remain strongly
user-centric even when they include health-aware logic.

\subsection{Multi-stakeholder recommendation and
fairness}\label{multi-stakeholder-recommendation-and-fairness}

The multi-stakeholder recommendation literature provides the strongest
theoretical foundation for the proposed framework because it explicitly
addresses the coordination of multiple legitimate utilities within a
shared decision space (Zheng 2017; Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019; {Wu et
al.} 2021). This stream argues that recommendation environments often
involve several affected parties and that recommendation quality should
therefore be evaluated across multiple utilities rather than only
end-user satisfaction.

\subsection{Sustainability and food-service
operations}\label{sustainability-and-food-service-operations}

The sustainability and institutional food-service literature emphasizes
food waste, restaurant sustainability, and operational coordination,
thereby highlighting the need to connect environmental performance with
service design and execution ({Peixoto et al.} 2026; {Guimaraes et al.}
2024). This stream is especially relevant for canteen planning,
procurement, and waste reduction, although it is still only weakly
connected to healthy-food recommender system design.

\section{Review Synthesis and Research
Gap}\label{review-synthesis-and-research-gap}

The reviewed literature can be synthesized into four interrelated
streams: \textbf{campus food environments}, \textbf{food and
healthy-food recommender systems}, \textbf{multi-stakeholder
recommendation}, and \textbf{sustainability and food-service
operations}. Taken together, these streams strongly support the
relevance of the proposed topic, but they also show that the field
remains fragmented and that an integrated university-campus framework is
still underdeveloped ({Bondevik et al.} 2024; {Caruso et al.} 2025;
Zheng 2017; {Peixoto et al.} 2026).

\subsection{1. Campus food environments as intervention
settings}\label{campus-food-environments-as-intervention-settings}

A consistent finding across campus food studies is that university food
environments shape dietary behavior through factors such as
availability, affordability, convenience, time pressure, and social
context (Roy et al. 2015; Deliens et al. 2014; {Caruso et al.} 2025).
This means that healthy eating on campus cannot be reduced to individual
preference alone. Any recommendation framework intended for university
campuses must therefore be embedded in the actual food environment
rather than designed as an isolated personalization tool.

\subsection{2. Food recommender systems are mature, but mainly
user-centric}\label{food-recommender-systems-are-mature-but-mainly-user-centric}

The food recommender systems literature is now sufficiently mature to
support domain-specific design choices ({Bondevik et al.} 2024; Tran et
al. 2018; Trattner and Elsweiler 2017). However, most existing systems
remain centered on the individual user. Even when they incorporate
health-aware logic, they usually optimize for some combination of user
preference, dietary fit, and nutritional value, while paying limited
attention to food-service feasibility, supplier constraints,
institutional objectives, or environmental outcomes.

\subsection{3. Multi-stakeholder recommendation offers the strongest
theoretical
foundation}\label{multi-stakeholder-recommendation-offers-the-strongest-theoretical-foundation}

The multi-stakeholder recommendation literature provides the clearest
conceptual basis for the proposed healthy-food ecosystem (Zheng 2017;
Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019; {Wu et al.} 2021). This literature is
highly relevant because campus food systems are inherently multi-actor
environments. Consumers seek taste, convenience, affordability, and
health; canteens seek manageable production and waste reduction;
suppliers seek predictable demand; and administrators may prioritize
sustainability and wellbeing.

\subsection{4. Sustainability and food-service research remain
insufficiently integrated with
recommendation}\label{sustainability-and-food-service-research-remain-insufficiently-integrated-with-recommendation}

The sustainability and food-service literature adds an important
operational perspective ({Peixoto et al.} 2026; {Guimaraes et al.}
2024). Research in this stream still focuses heavily on waste reduction,
especially post-distribution and plate waste. While this is important,
the literature provides weaker integration between sustainability
metrics, nutritional goals, and recommendation-system design.

\subsection{5. Explainability, trust, and real-world deployment are
emerging
priorities}\label{explainability-trust-and-real-world-deployment-are-emerging-priorities}

Newer implementation-oriented studies indicate that healthy-food
recommendation is moving beyond technical prototypes toward real-life
settings (Authors 2025; {Wang et al.} 2024; {Rostami et al.} 2024). This
has direct implications for campus adoption. A university healthy-food
ecosystem would likely require not only accurate recommendation, but
also interpretable suggestions, stakeholder buy-in, and policy
legitimacy.

\subsection{6. Overall synthesis and
gap}\label{overall-synthesis-and-gap}

Across the reviewed streams, the literature supports three major
conclusions ({Bondevik et al.} 2024; {Caruso et al.} 2025; Zheng 2017;
{Peixoto et al.} 2026). First, the campus is a valid and influential
food environment. Second, healthy-food recommendation is technically
feasible and increasingly sophisticated. Third, multi-stakeholder
recommendation provides an appropriate framework for balancing competing
interests. What remains missing is an integrated model that combines
these insights into a single university-campus food ecosystem connecting
consumers, canteens, providers, suppliers, researchers, and
administrators.

\section{Theoretical Framework and
Propositions}\label{theoretical-framework-and-propositions}

\subsection{Theoretical framing}\label{theoretical-framing}

Mission-oriented services differ from conventional service systems
because they are designed to achieve not only market or organizational
performance, but also broader societal and institutional goals such as
sustainability, health, inclusion, resilience, and wellbeing. In such
systems, value cannot be adequately explained through dyadic
provider-consumer exchange alone. Instead, value emerges through
interactions among multiple actors whose interests are distinct,
partially aligned, and interdependent. This requires a broader
theoretical perspective that combines \textbf{value co-creation},
\textbf{service ecosystems}, and \textbf{multi-stakeholder
coordination}.

The framework proposed in this study is grounded in three complementary
ideas. First, value co-creation theory suggests that value is created
through interaction, resource integration, and mutual adjustment among
actors rather than transferred unilaterally from producer to user.
Second, service ecosystem theory emphasizes that value creation occurs
within institutionalized structures of actors, rules, technologies, and
resource flows. Third, multi-stakeholder recommendation logic provides a
mechanism for operationalizing coordination across heterogeneous actors
by explicitly modeling their utilities and balancing them within a
shared decision framework.

Bringing these perspectives together, this study argues that
mission-oriented services can be understood as \textbf{sustainable value
co-creation ecosystems} in which consumers, producers, regulators, and
operators jointly contribute to both stakeholder-specific and
mission-level outcomes. Within this ecosystem, the
\textbf{Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS)} acts as the
coordinating intelligence that aligns decentralized choices with broader
mission goals.

\subsection{Core constructs}\label{core-constructs}

\subsubsection{Mission-oriented service
ecosystem}\label{mission-oriented-service-ecosystem}

A mission-oriented service ecosystem is defined here as a structured
network of interacting actors, resources, rules, and technologies
organized around a shared public or strategic objective. Unlike ordinary
service systems, mission-oriented ecosystems must balance private
interests with public outcomes.

\subsubsection{Stakeholder value
co-creation}\label{stakeholder-value-co-creation}

Stakeholder value co-creation refers to the process by which consumers,
producers, regulators, and operators jointly generate value through
interdependent exchanges of information, resources, constraints,
incentives, and feedback. In this view, value is not created by one
actor and consumed by another; it is co-produced through coordination.

\subsubsection{Exchange mechanism}\label{exchange-mechanism}

The exchange mechanism is the set of structured flows through which
stakeholders interact. These flows include demand exchange, supply
exchange, operational exchange, regulatory exchange, and feedback
exchange. These exchanges are the channels through which co-creation
becomes actionable.

\subsubsection{Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation
System}\label{multi-stakeholder-recommendation-system}

The MSRS is conceptualized as a decision-orchestration mechanism that
evaluates alternatives across multiple stakeholder utilities
simultaneously. It generates differentiated recommendations for
different actor groups and therefore supports mission alignment, not
only personalization.

\subsubsection{Mission performance}\label{mission-performance}

Mission performance refers to the degree to which the ecosystem achieves
shared societal or institutional objectives, such as sustainability,
health, affordability, fairness, or resilience, while maintaining
stakeholder viability.

\subsection{Propositions}\label{propositions}

\textbf{Proposition 1.} The effectiveness of mission-oriented services
increases when value creation is modeled as a multi-stakeholder
co-creation process rather than as a dyadic provider-consumer exchange.

\textbf{Proposition 2.} Structured exchange mechanisms among consumers,
producers, regulators, and operators positively influence value
co-creation in mission-oriented service ecosystems.

\textbf{Proposition 3.} The greater the alignment between
stakeholder-specific objectives and mission-level goals, the higher the
overall performance of the service ecosystem.

\textbf{Proposition 4.} An MSRS positively affects mission-oriented
ecosystem performance by improving coordination across stakeholder
exchanges.

\textbf{Proposition 5.} Compared with user-centric recommendation
systems, MSRS-based coordination leads to superior trade-offs among
quality, affordability, efficiency, and sustainability.

\textbf{Proposition 6.} The contribution of an MSRS to ecosystem
performance is strengthened when governance mechanisms such as fairness
rules, explainability, and mission-weighting are explicitly embedded in
the system.

\textbf{Proposition 7.} Mission-oriented service ecosystems supported by
MSRS are more likely to generate sustainable innovation outcomes than
ecosystems coordinated through static or rule-based mechanisms alone.

\subsection{Theoretical contribution}\label{theoretical-contribution}

The proposed theoretical framing contributes to engineering management
by extending value co-creation from a largely dyadic service logic to a
mission-oriented ecosystem perspective, by repositioning MSRS as a
mechanism for ecosystem-level coordination rather than only consumer
personalization, and by linking mission-oriented innovation with
digitally enabled governance of service performance.

\subsection{Process Model for Co-Design and Co-Engineering in
Mission-Oriented Service
Ecosystems}\label{process-model-for-co-design-and-co-engineering-in-mission-oriented-service-ecosystems}

The proposed ecosystem can be understood as a \textbf{closed-loop
mission-oriented innovation process} in which stakeholders jointly shape
the design, production, delivery, use, and continuous improvement of
products or services. Rather than treating innovation as a linear
process controlled by a single provider, the framework conceptualizes it
as a multi-stakeholder cycle in which consumers, producers, operators,
and regulators continuously exchange information, constraints,
capabilities, and feedback. Within this process, the
\textbf{Multi-Stakeholder Recommendation System (MSRS)} acts as the
coordination mechanism that aligns stakeholder decisions with both
individual interests and mission-level goals.

The process begins with a \textbf{sensing stage}, in which the ecosystem
collects information about consumer needs, usage contexts, price
sensitivity, quality expectations, and profiles, alongside producer
capabilities, operator capacities, and regulatory requirements. At this
stage, the purpose is not yet to produce a final offering, but to
construct a shared opportunity space in which innovation problems and
possibilities can be identified. The MSRS aggregates and interprets
these heterogeneous signals to identify promising directions for
ecosystem-level action.

The second stage is \textbf{co-design}, in which candidate products or
services are defined collaboratively. In this stage, consumers
contribute information about desirable attributes, producers contribute
design alternatives and innovation possibilities, operators identify
delivery and execution constraints, and regulators ensure that concepts
remain aligned with standards and mission objectives. The output of this
stage is not a single final offering, but a portfolio of feasible
concepts that can be evaluated against stakeholder utilities. The MSRS
contributes by ranking these alternatives according to expected consumer
relevance, producer feasibility, operator practicality, and regulatory
alignment.

The third stage is \textbf{co-engineering}, in which promising concepts
are transformed into feasible product-service configurations. Here, the
ecosystem moves from ``what should be offered'' to ``how it should be
produced and delivered.'' Producers determine feasible production
structures, operators design service and delivery workflows, regulators
verify compliance and performance standards, and consumers validate
expected usability and convenience. In this stage, the MSRS functions as
an engineering coordination tool by identifying the combination of
product attributes, resource choices, operational settings, and service
conditions that best satisfies ecosystem-level objectives.

The fourth stage is \textbf{production and resource allocation}, in
which the selected offering is prepared for execution. This includes
sourcing materials, assigning production capacity, scheduling
operations, and balancing cost and quality constraints. Although
producers and operators play the most visible roles in this stage,
consumer demand signals, regulatory constraints, and MSRS-guided
forecasts continue to shape decisions. The purpose of this stage is to
ensure that the offering is not only desirable in principle, but also
producible within available capabilities and constraints.

The fifth stage is \textbf{delivery and service execution}, in which the
offering is made accessible to consumers in a timely, convenient, and
reliable manner. This stage is especially important because
mission-oriented value can fail even when design quality is high if
service execution is poor. Operators are central here, but producers
support quality continuity, regulators monitor service standards, and
consumers directly experience convenience and timeliness. The MSRS
contributes by coordinating timing, availability, and service channels
so that the final delivery configuration remains aligned with
stakeholder conditions.

The sixth stage is \textbf{consumption and value realization}, where the
offering is actually used, experienced, and evaluated. At this point,
value is no longer only potential; it becomes realized through actual
usage. Consumers generate satisfaction, dissatisfaction, adoption, or
rejection; producers observe acceptance and innovation success;
operators observe service outcomes; and regulators observe
mission-related impacts. The ecosystem therefore treats consumption not
as the end of the process, but as a key information-generating stage.

The seventh stage is \textbf{evaluation}, in which stakeholder outcomes
and mission outcomes are assessed together. Performance in this stage is
measured not only in terms of user satisfaction, but also in terms of
affordability, operational efficiency, compliance, sustainability, and
innovation success. The MSRS integrates these outcomes into a
multi-stakeholder performance profile that reveals both synergies and
trade-offs. This enables the ecosystem to evaluate whether value has
been jointly created or whether one stakeholder's gain has come at the
expense of another.

The final stage is \textbf{reconfiguration and improvement}, in which
the ecosystem adapts based on observed results. Offerings may be
redesigned, production processes improved, delivery models adjusted,
incentives recalibrated, or recommendation logic updated. In this sense,
the ecosystem is not static; it is a continuously learning and
co-evolving system. The MSRS supports this adaptive process by updating
its coordination logic according to feedback from previous cycles.

Taken together, this process model shows that mission-oriented
innovation in service ecosystems is fundamentally \textbf{iterative,
multi-actor, and feedback-driven}. Consumers shape demand and realized
value, producers shape feasible offerings, operators shape execution
quality, and regulators shape mission compliance and public legitimacy.
The MSRS links these actors across the full lifecycle---from sensing and
design to production, delivery, consumption, evaluation, and
redesign---thereby enabling sustainable value co-creation rather than
isolated optimization.

\section{Evaluation Design}\label{evaluation-design}

\subsection{Methodology}\label{methodology}

\subsubsection{Research design}\label{research-design}

This study adopts a \textbf{design science and simulation-based
research} approach to develop and evaluate a \textbf{multi-stakeholder
healthy-food ecosystem} for university campuses. The proposed artifact
is an integrated socio-technical system composed of four tightly
connected layers: \textbf{stakeholders}, \textbf{food flow},
\textbf{decision support}, and a \textbf{multi-stakeholder
recommendation system (MSRS)}. For experimental evaluation, the
ecosystem is specified as a \textbf{multi-agent system (MAS)} in which
autonomous stakeholders interact within a shared campus food-service
environment. Formally, the system may be represented as
\(MAS = (A, E, F, O, P, U, T)\), where \(A\) denotes the set of agents,
\(E\) the environment, \(F\) the food-flow states and transitions, \(O\)
the observations available to each agent, \(P\) the decision policies,
\(U\) the stakeholder-specific utility functions, and \(T\) the temporal
transition rules. The agent set includes consumers, canteens, suppliers,
research/policy actors, administrators, and an MSRS coordinator. The
environment contains menus, inventory, kitchen capacity, supplier
pipelines, prices, queues, waste, and policy settings, while food
entities move through state-based transitions from raw ingredients to
preparation, serving, consumption, and waste. Each agent observes a
subset of the system state and chooses actions according to its
objectives and constraints. Consumer agents maximize taste, health,
affordability, and convenience; canteen agents maximize margin,
feasibility, and service quality while minimizing waste; supplier agents
maximize order stability and fulfillment performance; and administrative
actors maximize health, sustainability, fairness, and affordability
outcomes. The MSRS coordinator agent ranks candidate actions using a
weighted ecosystem objective and generates coordinated recommendations
for meal choice, menu planning, procurement, pricing, and interventions.
This framing is appropriate because multi-stakeholder recommendation
research treats recommendation as a setting with multiple legitimate
utilities, while agrifood digital-twin and simulation literature
supports virtual experimentation for food-flow, operational, and
sustainability decisions (Zheng 2017; Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019; {Wu
et al.} 2021).

\subsubsection{Ecosystem architecture}\label{ecosystem-architecture}

The ecosystem is modeled as a layered architecture:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Stakeholder layer}
\item
  \textbf{Food-flow layer}
\item
  \textbf{Decision-support layer}
\item
  \textbf{MSRS optimization layer}
\item
  \textbf{Governance and monitoring layer}
\end{enumerate}

This layered structure integrates physical food operations with digital
intelligence and coordinated decision-making, thereby linking ecosystem
design with operational execution (Zheng 2017; {Wu et al.} 2021).

\subsubsection{Stakeholder model}\label{stakeholder-model}

The stakeholder layer encompasses the following actor groups:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Consumers:} students, faculty, and staff
\item
  \textbf{Food-service operators:} canteens, caterers, kitchen managers
\item
  \textbf{Supply actors:} raw-food suppliers, processors, distributors
\item
  \textbf{Governance actors:} nutrition researchers, sustainability
  office, university administration
\end{itemize}

Each stakeholder group \(s\) is represented by: - a utility function
\(U_s\), - constraints \(C_s\), - decisions \(D_s\), - and performance
measures \(K_s\).

Typical utility structures include: - consumer utility: taste,
affordability, health, convenience; - canteen utility: margin,
feasibility, throughput, waste reduction; - supplier utility: demand
stability, order visibility, spoilage reduction; - university utility:
health promotion, sustainability, fairness, and policy compliance.

\subsubsection{Food-flow model}\label{food-flow-model}

The food system is represented as a directed network:

\[
G = (N,E)
\]

where \(N\) denotes process nodes and \(E\) denotes flow links. The main
food-flow chain is:

\textbf{Raw material supply → inbound logistics → storage →
preprocessing → recipe assembly → cooking/preparation → serving/display
→ purchase → consumption → leftovers/returns → waste or recovery}

Each food entity is characterized by: - quantity, - unit cost, - lead
time, - shelf life, - nutritional profile, - expected taste score, -
sustainability score or carbon footprint, - preparation time, -
spoilage/waste risk, - selling price.

\subsubsection{MSRS model}\label{msrs-model}

The MSRS evaluates candidate actions \(a\) through a weighted
multi-stakeholder objective:

\[
Score(a)=\sum_s w_s U_s(a)
\]

where: - \(U_s(a)\) is the utility of stakeholder \(s\) under action
\(a\), - \(w_s\) is the policy weight assigned to stakeholder \(s\).

The MSRS generates differentiated outputs for multiple actors: -
\textbf{Consumers:} meal recommendations, bundles, substitutions -
\textbf{Canteens:} prep quantities, menu mix adjustments, markdown
timing - \textbf{Suppliers:} replenishment plans, sourcing alternatives
- \textbf{Researchers/administrators:} nudges, labels, subsidy rules,
intervention placement

\subsubsection{Governance layer}\label{governance-layer}

A cross-cutting governance layer oversees the entire architecture
through: - fairness controls, - explainability, - consent and privacy
management, - policy-weight management, - KPI monitoring and
auditability.

To evaluate the proposed ecosystem, the study adopts a \textbf{hybrid

\begin{itemize}

\item

\item
  \textbf{System dynamics (SD)} for longer-term feedback loops such as
  demand-waste interactions and policy effects
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Overview of comparative
performance}\label{overview-of-comparative-performance}

To illustrate the managerial relevance of the proposed framework, we
report a set of \textbf{hypothetical simulation results} comparing four
scenarios: \textbf{no recommender}, \textbf{consumer-only recommender},
\textbf{rule-based healthy menu planning}, and the \textbf{full
MSRS-enabled ecosystem}. Across repeated simulation runs, the MSRS
scenario outperforms all baseline conditions on the majority of
ecosystem-level indicators while avoiding major losses for any
stakeholder group.

In the hypothetical results, the MSRS scenario increases \textbf{healthy
meal uptake} by approximately \textbf{18--26\%} relative to the
no-recommender baseline and by \textbf{8--14\%} relative to the
consumer-only recommender. At the same time, \textbf{taste satisfaction}
remains stable or improves slightly, suggesting that the system does not
achieve health gains by sacrificing perceived meal quality.
\textbf{Affordability compliance} also improves because the
decision-support layer uses pricing, bundling, and substitution logic to
keep recommended meals within student budget constraints.

\subsection{Operational and sustainability
results}\label{operational-and-sustainability-results}

Operationally, the MSRS scenario yields improvements in \textbf{forecast
accuracy}, \textbf{stockout reduction}, and \textbf{food waste
minimization}. In the hypothetical simulation, forecast accuracy
improves from roughly \textbf{72--78\%} in baseline settings to
\textbf{84--89\%} under the full ecosystem model. Plate and production
waste fall by \textbf{12--21\%}, primarily because the system aligns
menu planning, demand forecasting, and procurement more effectively.
Queue time remains within acceptable limits, indicating that healthier
and more diversified offerings do not create excessive service
bottlenecks.

The environmental results are also favorable. The MSRS scenario lowers
the \textbf{carbon footprint per meal} through more targeted sourcing,
better menu composition, and lower waste. In the hypothetical runs,
carbon intensity declines by \textbf{9--16\%}, while the share of
\textbf{local/seasonal sourcing} rises under scenarios where
sustainability receives moderate or high policy weight.

\subsection{Stakeholder utility
results}\label{stakeholder-utility-results}

The simulated stakeholder-utility analysis shows that the full MSRS
model produces the most balanced outcome across actors. Consumers gain
through improved choice relevance, health fit, and affordability;
canteens gain through waste reduction and better production planning;
suppliers gain through lower order volatility and better visibility; and
administrators gain through improved health and sustainability KPIs.
Although some scenarios produce small trade-offs, the MSRS condition
dominates baseline approaches on the combined ecosystem performance
score and produces a more favorable Pareto frontier across
\textbf{health}, \textbf{taste}, \textbf{price}, and \textbf{waste}.

\section{Discussion and Engineering Management
Implications}\label{discussion-and-engineering-management-implications}

\subsection{Engineering management
implications}\label{engineering-management-implications}

The hypothetical findings suggest that the proposed framework is highly
relevant to engineering management because it shifts the design problem
from isolated optimization to \textbf{ecosystem orchestration}. Rather
than treating food-service performance as the outcome of a single actor
or department, the framework models value as co-created through
coordinated decisions among consumers, operators, suppliers, and
governance actors. From an engineering management perspective, this is
important because many mission-oriented service systems require
cross-boundary coordination, dynamic trade-off management, and
performance measurement across multiple objectives rather than a single
efficiency metric.

\subsection{Value co-creation as a design
problem}\label{value-co-creation-as-a-design-problem}

The results also support the argument that \textbf{value co-creation}
should be treated as an operationalizable design logic rather than
merely a conceptual descriptor. In the proposed model, co-creation
emerges when coordinated recommendations improve stakeholder utilities
simultaneously: healthier and tastier meals are chosen by consumers,
waste and uncertainty are reduced for canteens, procurement becomes more
stable for suppliers, and institutional goals are advanced for
administrators. This extends value co-creation into a form that can be
modeled, simulated, and managed.

\subsection{Why MSRS adds value beyond consumer
personalization}\label{why-msrs-adds-value-beyond-consumer-personalization}

A key implication of the comparative scenarios is that a
\textbf{consumer-only recommender} is not sufficient for
mission-oriented sustainable systems. Although such a recommender
improves relevance at the user level, it does not necessarily improve
supply stability, waste, or ecosystem-wide efficiency. The MSRS creates
added value because it embeds recommendation inside a broader
decision-support architecture that links demand, menu design,
procurement, pricing, and sustainability. Thus, the main managerial
contribution of the MSRS is not only better personalization, but better
\textbf{coordination across stakeholder interfaces}.

\subsection{Broader transferability}\label{broader-transferability}

Although the present application focuses on campus food services, the
framework is transferable to other service ecosystems in which multiple
actors co-create value around mission-oriented outcomes. Examples
include hospital food services, workplace catering, school meal systems,
community health provisioning, and other sustainability-oriented service
platforms. More broadly, the model offers an engineering management
template for designing digital coordination systems in settings where
quality, affordability, operational efficiency, and sustainability must
be balanced simultaneously.

\subsection{Managerial implications}\label{managerial-implications}

For engineering managers, the framework suggests that mission-oriented
service innovation should be managed as an ecosystem orchestration
problem rather than as an isolated product or operations problem.
Managers should explicitly model stakeholder objectives, make exchange
mechanisms visible, and use digital coordination tools such as MSRS to
align design, sourcing, execution, and governance decisions. For
operators, this means using recommendation intelligence not only to
influence demand, but also to guide production scheduling, inventory
planning, and service delivery. For regulators and institutional
leaders, the framework highlights the importance of setting mission
weights, fairness safeguards, and incentive mechanisms that shape
ecosystem behavior without undermining stakeholder viability.

\subsection{Governance implications}\label{governance-implications}

The manuscript also implies that governance is a core design variable in
mission-oriented ecosystems. Recommendation systems operating across
multiple actors require transparent stakeholder weighting,
accountability for trade-offs, and explicit mechanisms for fairness,
explainability, and consent. From a TEM perspective, this means the
digital coordination layer should be designed not only for efficiency,
but also for legitimacy and institutional trust.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

This study is primarily conceptual and simulation-oriented. The campus
food-service setting serves as an illustrative application rather than a
fully validated empirical case study. The hypothetical results are
intended to demonstrate the type of system-level effects that the
framework could generate under realistic assumptions, but they do not
substitute for field implementation and empirical testing. In addition,
stakeholder utility weights are likely to vary across service ecosystems
and may be politically contested in practice. \# Conclusion

This paper developed a value co-creation modeling framework for
\textbf{mission-oriented sustainable service ecosystems} and illustrated
its application to \textbf{healthy, tasty, and affordable campus food
services}. The central contribution is the integration of stakeholder
modeling, food-flow modeling, decision support, and multi-stakeholder
recommendation into a single engineering management architecture. In
this formulation, the MSRS is not merely a personalization engine, but a
coordination mechanism that helps align consumer preferences,
operational feasibility, supplier stability, affordability, and
sustainability.

The paper contributes to engineering management by showing how
mission-oriented ecosystems can be designed and governed as
multi-stakeholder systems with measurable value co-creation outcomes. It
also demonstrates how recommendation systems can be reframed from
user-centric tools into broader mechanisms for ecosystem governance and
performance improvement. The proposed framework provides a structured
basis for future empirical work, simulation experiments, and field
implementation in campus food services and analogous service ecosystems.

\section{Future Research Directions}\label{future-research-directions}

Future research should extend and refine the present framework in five
directions. First, the model should be implemented as a full
\textbf{simulation environment or digital twin} in order to test
sensitivity to demand volatility, policy weighting, supply disruptions,
and consumer heterogeneity. Second, future studies should estimate
stakeholder utility functions empirically using transaction data, survey
data, interviews, and operational records. Third, field studies should
examine whether the framework improves outcomes in real campus dining
settings and whether observed gains are robust over time.

Fourth, future work should examine governance issues in greater depth,
particularly fairness across user groups, explainability of
recommendations, and the managerial legitimacy of stakeholder-weight
assignments. Finally, the framework should be generalized beyond campus
food services to other sustainable service ecosystems in which
mission-oriented innovation depends on coordinated digital decision
support. Together, these directions would move the framework from
conceptual and simulation-based design toward validated engineering
management practice.
